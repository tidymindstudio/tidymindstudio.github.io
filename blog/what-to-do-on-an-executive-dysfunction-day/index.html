What to Do on an Executive Dysfunction Day

September 14, 2026

An executive dysfunction day is the kind where you know exactly what you need to do, you want to do it, nothing is stopping you, and you still cannot start. You are not lazy and you are not procrastinating in the ordinary sense. The mechanism that converts intention into action is offline, and pushing harder on intention does not turn it back on.

What helps is having decided in advance what a day like this looks like, so you are not designing a plan with the exact faculty that is currently missing.

The bare minimum day

Most people only have one version of a day: the full one. Twelve things on the list, all of them assumed. When the day turns out to be an executive dysfunction day, the full version is unreachable, so you get zero out of twelve, and then you spend the evening feeling like the day was a write-off, which makes tomorrow harder.

So define a second version in advance. Call it the bare minimum day. It has exactly three parts:

  1. One unskippable task.
  2. One tiny win.
  3. A basics checklist.

That is the entire day. Not a reduced version of your normal list. A different, complete, finishable day. When you hit those three, the day is done - genuinely done, not done-with-an-asterisk.

One unskippable task

Pick the one thing that will cause real damage if it does not happen today. Not the most important thing in your life. The thing with a consequence attached to today specifically.

Usually it is something like: take your medication, send the one email that unblocks another person, pay the bill that is due, pick up the kid, feed the cat, submit the assignment. Almost everything else - the tidying, the admin, the thing you have been meaning to do for three weeks - can move to tomorrow without anything actually breaking.

Write it down. One line. When it is written, do two more things:

Shrink it until starting is trivial. If the task is “do the assignment,” the written task is “open the document and write one sentence.” If it is “call the doctor,” it is “find the number.” Starting is the broken step, so the written version should be the smallest thing that counts as started.

One tiny win

The second slot is one small, visible, physical thing that makes your immediate environment slightly better. Under five minutes, no decisions.

Take the dishes to the kitchen. Put the clothes on the chair into the hamper. Take the bin out. Wipe one counter. Open the curtains. Refill the water bottle.

This is not productivity theatre. It does two specific things. It gives you evidence that you can still affect the world, which matters on a day when you feel like you cannot. And it leaves you with an environment that is marginally less demoralising, which slightly lowers the cost of the next thing you try.

If you want a reliable one, the five-step room reset in how to clean your room with ADHD works fine cut down to a single step. Just the trash bag. Just the dishes. That counts.

The basics checklist

On a bad day the things that keep you functional are the first ones to silently drop out. Not on purpose - you just do not notice. Then at 4pm you feel dramatically worse and conclude something is deeply wrong with you, and the actual explanation is that you have had coffee and nothing else since morning.

So keep a short written list of the physical basics and tick them:

  • Medication, if you take it
  • Water
  • Something to eat, even if it is unimpressive
  • Out of yesterday's clothes
  • Teeth
  • Outside, or at least a window open, for a few minutes

The list exists because remembering it is itself executive function, and that is the thing that is down. On a good day you do not need the list. On this kind of day, reading a list is much cheaper than generating one.

Permission to do less on an executive dysfunction day

Here is the part that is hard to accept and does most of the work: on an executive dysfunction day, doing less is the correct strategy, not a concession.

When you cannot start, what usually happens is not rest. It is an all-day standoff. You sit with the full list, do none of it, feel worse, and burn the entire day in low-grade dread. You get zero tasks and zero recovery - the worst available outcome. Deciding early that today is a bare-minimum day ends the standoff. You get the one thing that mattered, plus actual rest.

The trick is to make it a decision instead of a collapse. Say it plainly, in the morning if you can tell, or at whatever point it becomes obvious: today is a bare-minimum day. Write the three things. Cross the rest off, physically, so your brain stops running background processes on them.

One more thing: name what you did, at the end. “Nothing” is almost never accurate. You probably ate, took a shower, answered a message, kept a person or an animal alive. On a day when the mechanism was offline, those were not free. Counting them honestly is not self-congratulation, it is accuracy, and accuracy is what stops one bad day from becoming a story about what you are like.

Tomorrow's page is a fresh start

The most useful property of a daily page is that it ends.

A bad day on a paper daily page is contained by the page. Tomorrow you turn it over and get a blank one. Nothing carries over except the tasks you deliberately move, and nothing on the new page says what happened yesterday.

This is why monthly grids and long rolling to-do lists are so rough on ADHD brains - they keep the evidence of a bad day in front of you for weeks, and turn one missed day into a visible streak of failure. The same mechanism ruins habit tracking, which is why most habit trackers fail ADHD brains: a grid with a gap in it stops reading as data and starts reading as a verdict.

You are allowed a certain number of bare-minimum days. More than you think. They do not compound into anything as long as each one ends at the bottom of its own page.
