ADHD Meal Planning That Actually Works

September 12, 2026

Most ADHD meal planning advice assumes the hard part is the planning. It is not. You can plan beautifully on a Sunday afternoon with a full stomach and a clear head. The part that breaks is 6pm on a Wednesday, when you are hungry, tired, out of decisions, and standing in front of a fridge full of ingredients that require you to have a plan you cannot currently access.

The 6pm decision problem

By early evening you have spent the entire day making small decisions. Executive function is worst exactly when dinner happens: late in the day, after work or school, often before food, sometimes after your medication has worn off.

And “what's for dinner” is not one decision. It is a stack of them. What do I want? What do we have? What needs using first? How long will it take? Do I have the energy for the pan I would have to wash after? Do I need to go to the shop? Every one of those is cheap on its own. Stacked together at the worst hour of your day, they cost more than you have, so you do the thing that costs nothing: you order food, or you eat crackers standing up, or you eat nothing and feel terrible at 10pm.

The goal is not to become a person with more evening willpower. The goal is to make 6pm require zero decisions.

ADHD meal planning means deciding once a week

Pick a slot once a week - fifteen minutes, on whatever day makes sense - and make all the dinner decisions then, in a batch, while your brain still works.

Write down what each night is. Not a recipe. Just a name: “pasta,” “tacos,” “stir-fry,” “soup and toast.” The point of writing it is not that you are committing to a gourmet week. It is that at 6pm you get to read the answer instead of generate it. Reading is nearly free. Generating is expensive.

Two rules make this survive contact with real life.

Don't assign meals to specific days if you can avoid it. Make a list of five dinners for the week and pick from the list each night. Fixed days create failure: miss Tuesday's plan and the whole week feels broken. A list is just a list. Nothing on it can be late.

Plan five dinners, not seven. There will be leftovers, an unexpected takeaway, a night you eat cereal. Planning seven guarantees you end the week with unused food and a small sense of failure. Planning five means you usually get it right, which is the feeling that keeps you doing it.

A master menu in three effort tiers

The single most useful thing you can make is a master menu: every dinner you actually know how to cook and would actually eat, written down once, sorted by effort. Not recipes you saved. Not things you want to be the kind of person who cooks. Things you have genuinely made more than twice.

Sort them into three tiers:

  • Low effort (under 10 minutes, one pan or none). Beans on toast. Scrambled eggs. Instant noodles with a frozen vegetable thrown in. A rotisserie chicken and bagged salad. Cereal, honestly.
  • Medium effort (20-30 minutes, some chopping). Pasta with a jar sauce plus something added. Stir-fry. Tacos from pre-cooked meat. Soup and grilled cheese. Fried rice from leftover rice.
  • High effort (45 minutes or more, or multiple steps). Anything from a recipe. Roast dinner. Something you have to look up.

The tiers exist so you can plan against your actual week rather than an idealised one. You know Tuesday is going to be bad. Put a low-effort meal on the list for it. You are not lowering your standards, you are matching the meal to the fuel you will have. A planned low-effort meal is a success. An abandoned high-effort meal is not.

Emergency meals are part of the plan

Every plan assumes a version of you that shows up. Some nights that version does not show up, and the plan has to have an answer for that night too, or the whole system gets abandoned the first time it fails.

So keep a written list of three to five emergency meals, and keep their ingredients in the house permanently. Rules for an emergency meal: shelf-stable or frozen, under 10 minutes, no chopping, no decisions, and something you will genuinely eat when you feel bad. Frozen pizza counts. Tinned soup counts. Pasta and a jar of sauce counts.

Label those ingredients in your head as off-limits for normal cooking. They are not dinner options, they are the parachute. The whole point is that they are there on the night you cannot think, and the night you cannot think is not the night to discover you used the emergency pasta on Monday.

This is the same logic as having a bare-minimum version of your day rather than only a full version - see what to do on an executive dysfunction day. A system that only works on good days is not a system, it is a hobby.

Why weekend whatever nights prevent abandonment

Put two nights a week on the plan with nothing assigned. Call them whatever nights. Leftovers, toast, a bowl of frozen dumplings, takeaway, cheese and crackers on the sofa. No plan, no guilt, no tracking.

This sounds like giving up. It is the opposite: it is the thing that keeps the plan alive.

Rigid seven-day plans collapse the moment reality touches them, and when a plan collapses most people do not adjust it, they abandon the whole idea and go back to the 6pm fridge stare. Built-in slack means the plan absorbs a bad night instead of breaking. You did not fail the plan on Friday. Friday was a whatever night. The plan is still running.

Putting it together

The whole system, in five lines:

  1. Write a master menu once, sorted into three effort tiers.
  2. Once a week, pick five dinners from it against how the week actually looks.
  3. Make one grocery list, organised by store section so shopping is one pass.
  4. Keep three emergency meals permanently stocked and off-limits.
  5. Leave two nights as whatever nights.

At 6pm you read a list instead of solving a problem. That is the entire trick, and it is enough.
