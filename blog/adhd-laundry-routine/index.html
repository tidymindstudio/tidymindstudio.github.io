An ADHD Laundry Routine That Survives the Transitions

September 18, 2026

ADHD laundry almost never fails at the washing. You are perfectly capable of putting a load in - that part takes ninety seconds and gives you a small hit of having done something. What fails is everything after: the load that sits wet overnight, the dryer that stays full for four days, the clean basket that lives on the floor for a week and slowly becomes indistinguishable from the dirty pile.

Why ADHD laundry fails at transitions, not washing

Laundry is not one task. It is four tasks separated by long gaps, and each gap requires you to spontaneously remember something that is currently invisible, behind a closed door, in another room.

  • Put the load in. (Easy.)
  • Wait 45 minutes, then move it to the dryer. (This is where it dies.)
  • Wait 60 minutes, then get it out. (This is where it dies.)
  • Put it away. (This is where it dies.)

Each of those waits is a transition, and transitions are precisely where ADHD attention drops things. You do not decide to leave the laundry. You get absorbed in something else, and nothing in your environment reminds you, because the washer is closed and quiet and the clothes are out of sight. Out of sight is not a small factor here - for a lot of ADHD brains, an object behind a door effectively stops existing.

By the time you remember, the wet load smells, so it needs rewashing, so the whole thing costs double, so you start avoiding it. That is the actual failure loop. It is not laziness, it is four unprompted handoffs in a row.

Fix the handoffs and laundry mostly stops being a problem.

Put an alarm on every handoff

The rule: you do not start a load without setting the alarm for the next step. The alarm is part of starting the load, the same way closing the door is.

So it goes like this:

  1. Load the washer. Before you walk away, set an alarm for the cycle length plus five minutes. Label it “wet clothes to dryer.”
  2. Alarm goes off. Move the load. Immediately set the next alarm for the dryer, labelled “clothes out of dryer.”
  3. Alarm goes off. Take the clothes out while they are still warm. Set a fifteen-minute alarm labelled “put clothes away.”

Label every alarm with the actual action. An unlabelled alarm just says “something,” and at that moment you will not have the context to reconstruct what. A labelled one is an instruction you can obey without thinking.

Two things make this stick. Use a loud alarm, not a gentle chime, and put it on the phone that is physically on you. And do not silence it from across the room - stand up first, then silence it. The dismiss-and-forget loop is real and it costs you the load.

Dump the clean clothes onto the bed

This is the single most effective trick in this article, and it is slightly ruthless.

When the dryer finishes, do not put the clothes in a basket. Carry them to your bedroom and dump them on the bed.

A basket is a stable resting place. Clothes can sit in a basket for eleven days without generating any pressure at all, because a basket full of clean clothes is not a problem, it is just furniture now. A bed covered in clothes is different. You cannot sleep in it. At some point tonight, you will have to deal with those clothes, and the deadline is enforced by something outside your motivation - namely being tired and wanting to lie down.

One caveat: only dump what you are actually going to deal with today. If you dump three loads on the bed at 10pm, you will sweep them onto the floor, and then you have made everything worse. One load, dumped in the afternoon or early evening.

Ugly folding is allowed

A surprising amount of laundry avoidance is actually folding avoidance, and folding avoidance is usually a standards problem.

So drop the standard, explicitly:

  • T-shirts folded in half twice, badly, are fine.
  • Socks do not need to be paired. A sock drawer is a sock bin. You will find a match.
  • Underwear, pyjamas, gym clothes: no folding at all. They go in a drawer in a heap.
  • Towels and sheets: fold roughly, shove in the cupboard.
  • Anything that wrinkles badly goes straight onto a hanger while warm, which is faster than folding anyway.

No chairdrobe

Every ADHD household has one: the chair, the exercise bike, the corner of the sofa, where clothes accumulate in a state between clean and dirty. It exists because there is a genuine gap in the system - you wore that jumper for two hours and it is not dirty, but it is also not crisp, and neither the hamper nor the drawer feels correct. So it goes on the chair. Then everything goes on the chair.

The problem is not the chair. It is the missing third category. So make the category real and give it a container:

One hook, or one small basket, for worn-but-not-dirty clothes. That is the whole fix. It has a strict capacity, and the capacity is the rule: when the hook is full, the oldest thing goes in the hamper. No sorting, no decisions, no evaluating.

Then enforce two more things. Everything else is binary - it goes in the hamper or in the drawer, immediately, on the way past. And the chair is a chair. If something ends up on it, it goes to the hamper on your next pass through the room, no matter how clean it is. It is cheaper to wash a clean jumper occasionally than to maintain a pile.

The whole routine

Set the alarm when you start the load. Set the next alarm at every handoff. Dump the clean load on the bed, not in a basket. Fold badly. Use one hook for the in-between clothes and put everything else away immediately.

None of this requires you to become more consistent. It requires alarms doing the remembering and a bed doing the enforcing.
